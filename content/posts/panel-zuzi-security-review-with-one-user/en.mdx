## Introduction

Wednesday evening, I’m coding another module for Zuzia’s Dashboard, a learning app for literally one regular user. In a second terminal I fire up my own, numbered security review, because that’s my habit for every sizable module. I ask myself why I need it when only one person logs in, not a thousand. A moment later the same review spits out a real data leak between accounts on a shared browser. Fuck. Now I know why.

This post is the story of how, in under 36 hours, an app that was supposed to be a one‑off export for a single exam grew into a full‑blown platform with accounts, a database, and an AI assistant, and how the numbered security review of that app caught three production‑grade bugs, even though “production” here means one person.

You’ll get three things:

1. **What happened in those 36 hours**, when a static export turned into Zuzia’s Dashboard, an app with accounts via Appwrite, an admin panel, and AI‑powered material questions,
2. **What the numbered security review actually caught (I1, I2, I3)**, with concrete commit hashes, not vague “fixed login a bit” notes,
3. **When that level of paranoia makes sense for a single‑user app, and when it’s just a waste of time**, because this post could easily be read as a manifesto urging everyone to do the same.

## How Zuzia’s Dashboard Came About

It started with something the repo simply calls “Session”: a one‑off, static export of university material for a single exam. It was supposed to live a week, maybe two. In the window I describe in this post I rebuilt it from scratch into something that should live longer. `docs/PROJEKT.md` says it straight out in one sentence I left for myself so I wouldn’t forget why I’m doing this:

> A personal learning tool for Zuzia, for the whole degree, not just one retake.

Zuzia’s Dashboard breaks lecture material into a tree of subjects and modules, and each module renders as a note, flashcards, quiz, matching pairs, and a checklist, with light gamification: points, streaks, seventeen badges. Sounds like a decent app. It is a decent app. The difference from the “Session” isn’t that I added more features, it’s that I stopped treating it as a one‑off.

## 36 Hours That Turned an Export into an App with Accounts

The first commit in the `sesja` repository lands at 2026‑09‑05T11:58Z. Less than 36 hours later, at 2026‑09‑06T23:46Z, the project already has user accounts via Appwrite OAuth, per‑user tables in the database, chat history, an admin panel, and an AI assistant powered by Groq for material‑related questions. That’s over 150 commits in a single weekend.

Fair warning: those timestamps are commit timestamps in a single repo, not a count of my hours at the keyboard. A good chunk of those commits are co‑authored by the “Claude Fable 5.1” session, i.e., agent sessions that worked on the code in parallel or while I was away. I don’t know, and I’m not claiming, to what extent those 36 hours were me at the laptop versus an agent doing its thing in the background. All I know is what the commit history shows, and that’s already enough material.

One detail of that pace that actually made me smile, because it shows I wasn’t flying blind: `README.md` explains how fact‑row IDs in the database are deterministic.

> Fact-row ids are deterministic (hash32(...natural keys)), so every write from lib/state/write.ts is an upsert-or-delete-by-id and a retried event can never double an effect.

In other words, even if something repeats, say a retry after a dropped connection, the app won’t duplicate your points for the same flashcard twice. I designed that consciously from the start, not as a later‑stage fix. The rest of this post is about what I **didn’t** design that way from the get‑go.

## Numbered Security Review of an App with a Single User

A quick disclaimer before anyone assumes I paid a firm for a flashcard‑app pentest: this wasn’t a third‑party audit or any automated scanner. It was my own, numbered review that I organized myself during the same weekend of building, with labels I1, I2, I3 baked straight into the commits. Just discipline, not a certificate.

**I1: a shared browser could swap data between accounts.** The event queue for a logged‑out user could be “replayed” on the next person who logs in on the same device. That’s exactly the “two people, one laptop” scenario, the classic case that a single‑user mental model most easily overlooks, because it assumes device = account.

> A shared browser could replay one logged-out user's queued state events into whoever logs in next

([40c9c1fc](https://github.com/bartosz-skejcik/sesja/commit/40c9c1fc))

**I2: a transient failure looked like an invalid session.** The `userOf()` function caught every error and returned `null`. Result: a brief network hiccup or an Appwrite restart was indistinguishable from an expired session, so the app cleared cookies and logged out *all* active users at once, even though nothing was actually wrong with their sessions.

> userOf() previously caught every error and answered null, which made a network blip or an Appwrite restart look identical to an invalid session: every active user got logged out and cookie‑cleared.

([8efc769c](https://github.com/bartosz-skejcik/sesja/commit/8efc769c))

**I3: the fix for I1 introduced its own hole.** This is the moment I love most, because it shows a numbered review actually *checks* something rather than becoming a checklist. After the I1 fix, `box()` threw an error when `currentUserId` was empty. But `dispatch()` still applied optimistic state and queued the event unconditionally, so the thrown error didn’t stop anything downstream ([d34d4cdb](https://github.com/bartosz-skejcik/sesja/commit/d34d4cdb)). I patched the hole and, in the process, learned that “throwing an error” and “stopping the operation” are two different things if nobody checks what happens to that error later.

## Why an App for One Person Got Treated Like It Was for a Thousand

Because risk doesn’t scale with the number of accounts, it scales with what the app holds and where it lives. Zuzia’s Dashboard has accounts, per‑user data, and realistically runs on a device that’s often shared within a family. That’s enough reason to treat a cross‑account leak as a real problem, not a theoretical curiosity from a security article.

I don’t know the exact number of accounts that exist today, other than there being one regular user and the possibility that a family member might launch the app on the same hardware. It’s not a SaaS that scales, and I’m not pretending it does. But I1 shows precisely why the number of accounts is a poor risk metric: two people on one device are enough for a data leak to become *real*, not hypothetical.

## When Such Paranoia for a Single‑User App Is a Waste of Time

Conversely, it’s easy to read this as “always do a numbered security review even for a two‑person app”. That’s not always sensible. Before you copy this process to your own project, check whether you actually have the same problem. Skip the numbered review if:

- the app has no accounts and stores nothing per user, so there’s nothing to “leak” between users,
- the app runs locally on a single device that is never shared,
- the data in the app is public or irrelevant, so even a real leak costs nobody anything.

Zuzia’s Dashboard hits all three points at once: it has accounts via Appwrite, lives on a device that can be shared, and stores private data for a specific person: notes, quiz progress, AI chat history. If it were a login‑less, offline flashcard generator with no state persistence, the whole numbered review would be form over substance. Here it isn’t.

## What This Means

Zuzia’s Dashboard now has three confirmed and fixed bugs from that review. I’m not claiming those are *all* the issues that exist, because the review only checks what I happen to think of, not everything that exists. The irony is that the app has a single regular user, yet it wasn’t Zuzia who reported those three bugs. My own numbered review did, three times in a row, before I decided I could take a break from hunting. That probably says more about me than about the app itself.

## FAQ

**Is Zuzia’s Dashboard publicly available, in production?**  
This post doesn’t say, because that’s not part of the story. The focus is on the security process and what it uncovered, not where the app lives today.

**How many people actually use Zuzia’s Dashboard?**  
I don’t know the exact number. Realistically one regular user, plus the chance a family member might run the app on the same device. It’s definitely not an app for thousands of accounts, despite the title.

**Who performed the security review, an external firm?**  
No. It was my own, numbered review (I1, I2, I3), done the same weekend as the rest of the build. Not a formal audit, not a pentest, not an automated scanner. Discipline, not a certificate.

**After these three fixes is the app fully secure?**  
I don’t claim that. I caught three concrete, confirmed bugs. There could be others I haven’t seen yet, because a numbered review checks what I think of, not every possible issue.

**Why does a flashcard app need a Groq AI assistant?**  
To ask material‑related questions directly from the module, instead of hopping between the app and a separate chat. It’s one of the things that grew alongside accounts and the admin panel in those 36 hours.
